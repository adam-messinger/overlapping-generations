# Lessons from Italy (with Spain and Germany as controls)

Italy is the second-oldest large country on earth and — critically — the one big European economy
that aged *without* significant immigration. Spain aged just as fast but imported ~9.5M foreign-born
residents (1.4% → ~20% of population, 1996-2026). Germany aged and imported millions in waves
(euro-crisis, 2015, 2022). The three countries are therefore a natural experiment: same demographic
disease, three immigration dosages.

**Result:** Italian national house prices fell ~24% in *real* terms 2011-2024. Spain and Germany
boomed. Within all three, extreme spatial divergence.

## 1. Winners

| Place | Evidence | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Milan | +49% over the decade national prices fell in real terms; only major Italian city "significantly above average" | The one true agglomeration: finance, design, international capital, post-Expo investment |
| Bologna, Padua | Student rents +37% / +46% (2020-26); Bologna among Europe's costliest student cities | Universities with strong labor markets attached |
| Venice, Florence | Venice historic center: 175k residents (1950s) → 48k, yet prices soared; ~6M tourists vs <50k residents | **The tourism-value paradox**: external demand fully replaced resident demand |
| Lake Como, Garda, Tuscany prestige zones (Chianti, Val d'Orcia) | Top-20 global second-home index; Chianti €2,630/m² +4%/yr; Americans ~29% of foreign purchases in Tuscany | Scarce, prestigious, globally marketed amenity — priced off world wealth, not Italian demographics |
| Madrid | Largest population magnet in Spain (+226k in one year); prime districts +6-9%/yr | Capital + agglomeration + immigrant gateway |
| Málaga / Costa del Sol / Alicante / Balearics | >20% above 2007 bubble peak (one of few Spanish markets); foreign buyers 25-44% of transactions; tourist municipalities +105% since 2015 vs +67% elsewhere | International retirement/amenity migration — Europe's Florida |
| Munich | +153% (2008-19), most expensive German city for 20 years | Jobs + tight supply |
| Berlin | ~160% real gain since 2000 (best in Germany) | Capital functions + creative prestige + migration waves + chronic under-building |
| Leipzig | 437k (1998) → 616k; condos +250% (2010-22); only major German market above its 2022 peak in 2025 | **The turnaround case**: university + culture + cheap gründerzeit stock + industrial re-anchoring (BMW, Porsche, DHL) after demolishing 12,900 units |
| Freiburg, Heidelberg, Münster | Freiburg the only major German market that *rose* in the 2024 correction | University towns = downside resilience |

## 2. Losers

| Place | Evidence | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Genoa | -33% over a decade; population peak 1972 | Port-industrial decline + aging + no compensating institution — Italy's Yubari, at metro scale |
| Rome (-13%), Palermo (-21%), Turin (-5%), Naples (~flat nominal = deep real loss) | Three of Italy's four largest cities lost nominal value | Government presence without private dynamism did not hold values |
| Italian inner areas / Mezzogiorno interior | >70% of towns under 5,000 lost population over 20 years; €1-house towns (70+: Sambuca, Mussomeli, Ollolai…) | Market-clearing price of institutionless housing ≈ 0 (net of renovation, negative) |
| España vaciada (Teruel, Soria, Zamora, Cuenca, Jaén) | Rural Spain -4.4% population while nation +2.6%; only 4 provinces saw price declines during the 2021-23 national boom — all interior | No replacement engine; provincial capital status insufficient (Valladolid still ~15% below 2007) |
| Seseña, Valdeluz | Iconic 2008 ghost towns: 13,000 flats planned, ~5,000 built then abandoned | **Overbuild against phantom demand** — elastic supply into demographic headwind |
| East German shrinking cities (Hoyerswerda, Chemnitz, Halle, Schwerin) | Hoyerswerda halved (68k → <30k); Stadtumbau Ost demolished ~283k-300k units to stabilize markets; Chemnitz rents €6.40/m² vs Potsdam €13.20 | Industrial collapse + emigration; demolition as price support managed decline but did not reverse it |
| Gelsenkirchen / Ruhr periphery | Cheapest big-city housing in West Germany (~€1,605/m²) | 60 years of structural policy "have not healed" the coal/steel transition |

## 3. Mechanisms, ranked

1. **Agglomeration of the remaining young into 1-3 metros.** CaixaBank: Spanish municipalities gaining
   5-10k people saw 7.3%/yr price growth vs 3.4% in shrinking ones — a near-monotonic gradient. Italy
   is a one-winner country (Milan); polycentric Germany has several; the *number* of winners is set by
   the economy's structure, not by preference.
2. **International amenity/second-home/retirement demand** is the cleanest decoupling mechanism
   (Costa del Sol above bubble peak; Como; Tuscany). It selects for scarcity + prestige + climate +
   access, and it is exported from *other* countries' demographics and wealth.
3. **Immigration determines the national floor** (Spain 20% foreign-born → boom; Italy 8% → national
   real decline; Germany in between, with waves). Within countries, immigrants concentrate in winner
   metros and gateways, amplifying divergence.
4. **Universities amplify but do not guarantee** — Bologna/Padua/Freiburg/Leipzig vs Pisa (+16% while
   Padua +46%) and Halle (major university, persistent vacancy). University + jobs + attractive stock
   compounds; university alone does not.
5. **Supply elasticity is two-sided** (same as Japan/Korea): under-building amplified Munich/Berlin/
   Leipzig gains; overbuilding produced Seseña/Valdeluz and the East German 1.3M-unit vacancy that
   took 300k demolitions to stabilize.
6. **Government/capital functions are weaker than assumed**: Rome fell 13%; Valladolid never recovered
   2007; Berlin and Madrid won for reasons beyond government. (Contrast Japan's prefectural capitals —
   government helps most in *small* regional markets, not in metros.)
7. **High-speed rail mostly did NOT save small cities.** Spain built the world's 2nd-largest HSR
   network; España vaciada kept emptying; Valdeluz was built on an AVE-stop bet and collapsed. The one
   success mode: ≤1hr commuter extension of a booming capital (Ciudad Real). Same lesson as KTX-Daegu.
8. **Place-based revival policy has near-zero measured effect**: Bank of Italy diff-in-diff on the
   inner-areas strategy (SNAI): no effect on population, employment, or house prices. €1 houses are
   publicity, not revival. Stadtumbau Ost "succeeded" by destroying supply.

## 4. Surprises worth carrying to the US case

- **Capitals can lose** (Rome). Institutional *presence* is not institutional *demand growth*.
- **The Venice inversion**: resident collapse + price boom. Where external (tourist/second-home)
  demand substitutes for residents, population decline stops predicting value.
- **Recovery from the loser tier is possible when a booming metro overflows** (Seseña now refilling
  from Madrid's affordability crisis) — proximity to a winner is an option on the winner's success.
- **Leipzig vs Chemnitz, 80km apart, same state, opposite fates** — university + amenity stock +
  airport/industry beat shared regional demographics. Micro-selection matters more than region.
- **Interior provincial capitals with every "institutional" box ticked** (university, government,
  hospital — Valladolid, Zamora) still lost when the surrounding region emptied faster than the
  institutions could pull. Institutions must be net importers of people, not just present.
