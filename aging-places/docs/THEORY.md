# A Theory of Aging-Society Geography

*Why some places become more important as societies age, while others become less important.*

Derived from the experience of Japan, South Korea, Italy, Spain, and Germany (see
`LESSONS_JAPAN.md`, `LESSONS_ITALY.md`). The theory is stated as mechanisms, then as testable
propositions that the municipal scoring model (`METHODOLOGY.md`) operationalizes.

---

## 0. The central observation

Aging does not shrink demand uniformly; it **changes what demand is made of and concentrates it**.

In a growing society, demand for place comes mostly from *new household formation*, which is broadly
distributed — every suburb gets its share of the growth. In an aging society, three other demand
sources take over, and all three are *spiky*, not spread:

1. **The migration of the scarce young** (jobs, universities, urban amenity) — winner-take-most.
2. **The consumption of the accumulated wealth of the old** (second homes, retirement moves,
   medical proximity, bequests) — flows to amenity and service concentrations.
3. **External demand** (immigrants, foreign capital, tourists) — flows to gateways, prestige
   destinations, and global amenity assets.

Meanwhile the *elastic* component of housing demand — the marginal family that would have bought
anywhere — disappears. What remains is inelastic, choosy demand meeting a housing stock built for
the old geography. The result everywhere observed is **tripolarization** (Japan's 三極化): a small
rising tier, a broad sagging middle, and a large tail sliding toward zero (or negative,
demolition-liability) value.

## 1. The four capitals

A municipality's fate in an aging society is set by its endowment of four kinds of "capital," which
interact multiplicatively rather than additively.

### 1.1 Institutional Concentration (the replacement engines)

Universities, medical centers, government seats, military bases, research complexes, and regional
service monopolies share a property ordinary employers lack: **they import people on a schedule**.
A university imports 18-22 year olds every September regardless of the national birth rate (until it
can't — see failure mode). A medical hub imports both workers (disproportionately young women — the
exact demographic whose loss defines the Masuda "vanishing municipality" index) and elderly patients
with wealth. A capital imports civil servants; a base imports 20-somethings by order.

Evidence: Fukuoka/Sapporo/Sendai consolidating their hinterlands; prefectural capitals holding value;
Sejong capitalizing government announcements at ±40%; Bologna/Padua/Freiburg/Leipzig; Rochester MN
logic generally. Failure modes: institutions that stop importing (Japan's collapsing private
universities, Keisen closing inside Tokyo), and institutions embedded in an emptying region too weak
to offset it (Valladolid, Zamora, Rome). **Institutional presence is necessary throughput, not
stock: what protects value is the flow of people the institution imports each year.**

### 1.2 Amenity Concentration

Coasts, lakes, mountains, wine country, heritage cities — places where the wealthy *want to be*.
In an aging society this capital appreciates for two reasons: (a) the old hold most of the wealth
and buy amenity with it (second homes, retirement); (b) amenity demand is exportable — Niseko,
Como, Costa del Sol, and Tuscany are priced off *global* wealth, not national demographics.

Critical qualifier from the akiya belt (warm, coastal Wakayama/Shikoku at 20%+ vacancy) and from
"cheap scenic" everywhere: **amenity only stores value when combined with scarcity, prestige, or
access.** Amenity + elastic supply = Spanish coastal overbuild. Amenity + no access + no institutions
= akiya. Amenity + scarcity + prestige = Carmel, Como, Karuizawa.

### 1.3 Demographic Regeneration (replacement ratio)

The master variable. Not current size, not even current growth, but the **capacity to continuously
re-attract 20-44 year olds** as residents age in place. Everything else (institutions, amenity,
access) matters *through* this channel or through the wealth channel. The measurable core:

- ratio of 25-44s to 65+ (stock),
- inflow of young adults per resident aging into retirement (flow),
- presence of the engines that mechanically generate that inflow.

Two corollaries observed repeatedly: (a) a place can regenerate while shrinking in total (Toyama
center, Leipzig 1998-2005) — regeneration is compositional; (b) a place can grow while failing to
regenerate (retirement boomtowns absorbing one giant cohort — they replay Tama New Town / danchi
30 years later: single-cohort places age catastrophically, in sync).

### 1.4 Wealth Concentration

Aging societies concentrate wealth in the old, and the old spend it on: proximity to medicine,
downsized central housing near services, second homes in prestige amenity zones, and transfers to
children who buy in winner metros. Places positioned to *receive* elderly wealth (medical hubs,
amenity/prestige markets, high-service urban cores) gain purchasing power even with zero population
growth — the Venice inversion (residents ↓75%, prices ↑) is the limiting case. Places whose
housing the old must eventually *sell with no buyer behind them* (undifferentiated exurbs, aging
single-cohort suburbs) face the reverse flow.

## 2. Interaction laws

1. **Multiplicativity.** Winners score on ≥2 capitals (Fukuoka: institutions × regeneration;
   Karuizawa: amenity × access to Tokyo; Bologna: institutions × prestige). One-capital places are
   fragile: pure government (Rome), pure amenity (akiya coast), pure university (Halle, Pisa).
2. **Supply sets the amplitude, demand sets the sign.** Inelastic supply amplifies gains in winners
   (Munich, Seoul, Kyoto) and — crucially — *scarcity cannot save a demand loser* (constrained
   Genoa still fell 33%). Elastic supply moderates winner prices (Tokyo rents) and deepens loser
   crashes (Daegu, rural Korea >112% supply ratios, Seseña). A model must interact supply growth
   with predicted demand direction.
3. **Access is a multiplier with a threshold.** ≤~1hr to a booming core turns periphery into
   spillover (Ciudad Real, Karuizawa, Seseña's revival); beyond it, HSR/highways change nothing
   (España vaciada with AVE stops, Daegu with KTX). Transit is necessary-not-sufficient.
4. **External demand decouples the top tier from national demographics.** Immigration sets the
   national floor (Spain vs Italy) and immigrant gateways outperform; global amenity capital sets
   the top of the amenity tier.
5. **Momentum is real but exhaustible.** Places running on a single inbound cohort (retirement
   booms, bedroom exurbs of one factory era) look like winners on 20-year growth charts and then
   flip in sync. Structure (engines, replacement flows) beats momentum at 20-40 year horizons —
   this is why the model must not simply rank recent growth.
6. **Policy can concentrate but not conjure.** Compact-city policy (Toyama) and capital relocation
   (Sejong) move value *within* a region; revival subsidies without engines (€1 houses, SNAI,
   Stadtumbau) do not change trajectories.

## 3. Testable propositions (operationalized in the model)

- **P1 (Replacement ratio):** 25-44/65+ ratio and young-adult inflow proxies in year *t* predict
  relative value growth over *t*+20-25 better than population growth in *t*-10..*t*.
- **P2 (Engines):** University enrollment per capita, health-care employment share and scale,
  government/military employment share predict young-adult inflow and value resilience,
  *conditional on* regional access (P4).
- **P3 (Amenity × scarcity):** Seasonal/second-home share interacted with supply constraint predicts
  top-tier appreciation; seasonal share with elastic supply predicts boom-bust.
- **P4 (Market access threshold):** Population/GDP within ~60-90 minutes has a positive, threshold-
  shaped effect; remote places need standalone engines to survive.
- **P5 (Human capital):** Bachelor's/graduate attainment predicts both regeneration and wealth
  inflow (it is the common currency of all four capitals).
- **P6 (Overbuild):** High recent housing-unit growth relative to household growth predicts
  underperformance in aging markets — especially where P1 is weak.
- **P7 (Single-cohort trap):** Places whose age structure is dominated by one cohort (very high 45-64
  share with weak 25-34) underperform 20 years later even if currently prosperous.

## 4. What this predicts for the United States, 2025-2065

The US ages later and slower than Japan/Italy (immigration, higher fertility), so the national
floor is Spain-like, not Italy-like. But the *internal* geography should follow the same laws:

- Metro cores and university/medical anchors regenerate; undifferentiated exurbs and single-cohort
  retirement tracts replay Tama New Town.
- The Sun Belt splits: amenity+scarcity coastal/mountain markets keep appreciating; flat-land
  elastic-supply boomtowns see Daegu-style oversupply cycles as boomer in-migration decays.
- Small institutional cities (state capitals, university towns, regional medical centers) become
  the Fukuokas/Sapporos of their emptying hinterlands — relative winners in absolute-decline regions.
- The bottom tier — remote, engine-less, aging, overbuilt — drifts toward Japanese-style
  zero-value equilibrium, which the US has mostly not yet priced.
