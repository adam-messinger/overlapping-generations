# Lessons from Japan (and South Korea)

Japan is the world's most instructive real-estate laboratory for aging: population peaked ~128M in
2008, the working-age population peaked in the mid-1990s, and roughly half of its municipalities are
projected to lose most of their young women by 2040 (Masuda report, 2014: 896 of 1,799 municipalities
"at risk of vanishing"). South Korea is ~15 years behind Japan on the same demographic path but
compressing faster. What follows is what actually happened to property values, and why.

## 1. Aggregate decline, extreme internal divergence

- 2025 kouji-chika (official land prices): +2.7% nationally — the strongest year since 1992 — while
  vacant homes hit a record 9.0M units, 13.8% of the national stock (2023). Both things are true at
  once: the average rises while the median rural parcel decays toward zero.
- Japanese analysts describe **tripolarization (三極化)**: (a) the top ~10-15% of stock — central,
  station-adjacent, institution-rich — *rising*; (b) a broad middle gradually declining; (c) a large
  tail (15+ minutes from a station, no services) heading toward **zero or negative value** (demolition
  liability exceeds land value).
- Korea shows the same pattern at national scale: in the March 2026 official land price release, 8 of
  17 metro regions/provinces *declined* while Seoul's Han-River wards averaged +23%. The OECD rates
  Korea's big-city/small-city price gap as the widest among members.

**US translation:** national aging does not produce uniform decline. It produces a sharper gradient.
The right question is never "will US housing fall?" but "which tier of the tripolarization is this
municipality in?"

## 2. Winners

| Place | Evidence | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Central Tokyo (Minato, Chiyoda, Shibuya, bay wards) | New condos ¥111.8M avg (2024), records every year since 2021, first surpassed the 1990 bubble peak in 2021; commercial land +10.4% (2025) | Agglomeration of jobs + global capital + foreign residents (>70% of 23-ward population growth in 2024 was foreign nationals) |
| Fukuoka | Residential land +9.0% (2025), best of any major city, 13 straight years of gains | **Youth-magnet regional hub**: top-3 in net in-migration of young people, startup visa zone, cheap relative to Tokyo |
| Sapporo, Sendai, Hiroshima | +5.8% as a group, outpacing Tokyo in some segments | Regional core cities vacuum up their aging hinterlands' remaining young people |
| Kyoto | New condos +30.6% y/y (2024); machiya +36% 2020-25 | Heritage prestige + tourism + hard supply constraints (height/preservation rules) |
| Niseko/Kutchan | Repeated #1 national land-price gains (+30%/yr survey highs; Hirafu +70% 2020-25) | Global amenity capital — priced off foreign wealth, fully decoupled from Japanese demographics |
| Karuizawa | +12.1% (2025), +32.7% 2018-23; school enrollment *rising* | Amenity + remote work + Shinkansen access to Tokyo; villa town became a primary-residence town |
| Toyama center | Land values along LRT corridors rose in a shrinking city | Deliberate compact-city policy: services + transit concentration |
| Seoul (Gangnam, Han River belt) | Prices ~doubled 2017-21; Seongdong +29% (2026); Gangnam ~3x northern Seoul | Winner-take-all agglomeration + restrictive supply |
| Sejong | +42.3% (2020, national #1), crash 2021-24, resurge 2025 on relocation news | Pure government-function capitalization — in both directions |

## 3. Losers

| Place | Evidence | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Yubari | 120k → 6.4k people; municipal bankruptcy (2007); housing worthless | Single-industry (coal) collapse + no replacement engine — the terminal case |
| Rural akiya belt (Wakayama, Tokushima, Kochi, Ehime, Kagoshima ~20-21% vacancy) | Abandoned stock 2.1M → 3.85M (2003-23) | No institutions, no accessibility, emigration of women 20-39 (the Masuda variable) |
| Tama New Town and 1960s-70s commuter towns / danchi | Oldest districts >30% 65+; schools and its private university (Keisen, 2023) closed; far-from-station houses in the "zero value" tier | Built for one cohort, aged with it; housing product (stair-access danchi) obsolete; young households skip it for central condos |
| Tokyo's own periphery | ~900k vacant homes inside Tokyo (10.3%) | The losing tier exists *inside* the winning region — micro-location (station distance) decides |
| Daegu (Korea, 2.4M people) | 112 consecutive weeks of price declines through Jan 2026; construction approvals suspended | **Oversupply meets demographic outflow** — metro scale and KTX did not save it |
| Busan (3.3M) | Lost ~600k people since 1995; first metro city rated "at risk of extinction"; prices -5% (2024) | Youth exodus to capital region; port-industrial decline |
| Gunsan, Geoje | GM/shipyard closures hollowed housing demand within 2 years | Industrial monotowns replay Yubari at compressed speed |
| Rural Korean provinces | Housing supply *ratios highest* exactly where extinction risk is highest (Gyeongbuk 113%, Jeonnam 112% vs Seoul 94%) | Construction chased yesterday's population map; elastic supply amplifies decline |

## 4. Mechanisms, ranked by evidentiary strength

1. **Youth migration is the master variable.** Fertility is uniformly low — what differs is where the
   few 20-39 year olds move. The Masuda "vanishing municipality" index (change in women aged 20-39) is
   the single best predictor of municipal land-value death. Winners are youth importers, whatever their
   size (Fukuoka 1.6M, Karuizawa 20k).
2. **Station/transit proximity tripolarizes values.** Rent premiums of ¥30-80k/month within 5-7 min of
   a station; >15 min brings discounts and eventual worthlessness. But transit is necessary, not
   sufficient: Daegu has KTX and 112 weeks of decline.
3. **Supply policy sets the amplitude; migration sets the sign.** Tokyo's permissive zoning kept rents
   affordable while prices rose (deep elastic supply in the winner). Korea's dying provinces built
   >112% supply ratios — permissive supply in losers accelerates collapse. Seoul restricted supply in
   the winner — prices doubled.
4. **Global amenity demand fully decouples from national demographics** (Niseko, Kyoto, Karuizawa,
   Jeju 2011-16). Small absolute markets, extreme percentage moves.
5. **Government functions capitalize into land** (Sejong ±40% swings on announcements; prefectural
   capitals hold value far better than non-capital peers in the same prefecture).
6. **Universities are an amplifier, not an anchor.** Regional private universities are themselves
   dying (16 closures/admission halts since 2000; 47.5% under-enrolled 2022) — a university that
   cannot recruit students protects nothing (Keisen closed *inside* metro Tokyo). A university plus a
   labor market (Fukuoka) compounds.
7. **Medical access works mostly through the elderly's own migration**: wealthy empty-nesters sell
   suburban houses and buy central condos near hospitals — reinforcing center-vs-periphery divergence;
   loss of clinics is a documented trigger of danchi abandonment.
8. **Immigration is now the marginal buyer at the core** (majority of central-Tokyo household growth;
   Kawaguchi's new Chinatown revaluing depreciated inner suburbs).

## 5. Surprises worth carrying to the US case

- "Winning" is relative: Tokyo's real commercial land is still ~70% below the 1991 peak. A winner in
  an aging society can deliver mediocre absolute returns — the loser tail is where fortunes are lost.
- The akiya belt is **warm and coastal** (Wakayama, Shikoku, Kagoshima), not cold and northern.
  Climate amenity without institutions or access does not protect value.
- Second-tier hubs (Fukuoka, Sapporo) outperformed the megacity in percentage terms — hinterland
  consolidation is a growth strategy available only to the *dominant* city of a declining region.
- Elastic supply is a two-edged sword — it moderates winner prices (Tokyo) and deepens loser crashes
  (Daegu, rural Korea). Any US model must interact supply growth with demand direction.
- Policy can create local winners inside shrinking regions (Toyama's LRT corridors, Sejong), but only
  by concentrating — never by spreading.
