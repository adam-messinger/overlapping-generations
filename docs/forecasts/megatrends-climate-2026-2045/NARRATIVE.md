# The Coming Two Decades, 2026-2045: A Forecast Narrative

*As of 2026-11-09. A plain-language reading of this panel's pooled forecast; the numbers,
definitions and seat-level sources are in `REPORT.md`, `questions.md` and `seats/`. This file
is a snapshot of a document that is edited elsewhere.*

The next twenty years look more like a slower, more indebted version of today than like a
boom or a breakdown. A 24-seat forecasting panel puts 67% on that middle path to 2045, 20.5%
on an AI-led productivity boom and 12.5% on a sovereign-debt or stagflation crisis.

## The forecast in numbers

Four quantities carry the central forecast: growth slows a little, trade keeps pace with
output, short-term money stays cheap and public debt keeps climbing. The range is the panel's
10th to 90th percentile.

| Quantity | Recent level | Central forecast | Range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Real GDP per person, OECD plus China, growth per year 2026-2045 | 2.5% over 2005-2025 | 2.2% | 1.6% to 2.8% |
| World goods trade growth as a multiple of world GDP growth, 2026-2045 | 0.93 over 2010-2023 | 0.96 | 0.65 to 1.32 |
| Neutral real interest rate (US, euro area, Canada), average 2036-2045 | 0.73% in mid-2026 | 0.9% | -0.05% to 2.0% |
| Advanced-economy gross public debt in 2045, share of GDP | 108% in 2025 | 136% | 111% to 164% |

Five larger breaks from today were priced as long shots. Each is a specific, checkable event.

| Event | Probability | What it would take |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trade grows far slower than output (multiple under 0.75) and digital services, AI hardware and minerals pass 42% of world trade | 10% | Eighteen years near 0.67 after a strong start, plus a mix that is roughly 37-40% today |
| The neutral real rate averages 2% or more in 2036-2045 | 9% | The US near 2.6% and the euro area near 1.0%; they are 1.0% and 0.1% now |
| G7 budgets carry climate costs of 1.5% of GDP a year in 2040-2045 | 6% | About five times today's level of roughly 0.3% |
| The US and China exceed half of world output while the EU falls below 11.5% | 4% | Plus 7.7 points for the pair, from 42.3%, and minus 6.3 for the EU, from 17.8% |
| Three G7 countries adopt a formal yield cap or an automation tax worth 2% of GDP | 3% | A third country beyond Japan and the US, which alone clearly pass the debt test |

## Growth slows but does not stall

Output per person across the rich world and China grows about 2.2% a year to 2045. That is
down from 2.5% over the past two decades, and the whole slowdown is China's.

China's growth per person ran at 5.4% a year over 2015-2025
([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.MKTP.PP.KD)). The panel's teams put
it near 3.0-3.2% for the next twenty years, faster in the first decade than the second. The
OECD holds near 1.3%, about where it has been since 2005.

So the first decade is the better one: roughly 2.3-2.45% a year to 2035, then 2.0-2.1% to
2045. A stall below 1% a year in the second decade gets about 7% on one team's arithmetic.

Cheap solar power and AI are in this forecast as support, not as a break. US productivity has
grown 2.2% a year since late 2019, against 1.5% over 2007-2019
([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/prod2_11052026.htm)). That only matches the
average since 1947, and factory productivity rose just 0.4% over the past year.

A boom, defined as 2.5% or more a year in 2036-2045, gets about one chance in five. It would
need OECD growth per person near 2.3% a year. No decade since 1990 has reached that; the
best, the 1990s, managed 1.95%.

## Trade keeps pace with output

World goods trade grows about as fast as world output over the two decades, a multiple of
0.96. That is the pattern since the financial crisis: trade grew 2.5% a year and GDP 2.7%
over 2010-2023 ([WTO](https://www.wto.org/english/booksp_e/trade_outlook24_e.pdf)).

The window opens hot. The WTO expects trade volume to grow 3.9% in 2026 and 4.1% in 2027,
against GDP growth of 2.6% and 2.9%
([WTO, 8 October 2026](https://www.wto.org/english/news_e/news26_e/stat_08oct26_510_e.htm)).
AI hardware supplied 47% of the growth in trade value in the first half of 2026, and ten
exporters ship about 85% of it.

The panel reads that as one sector's investment cycle, not a new era. The WTO's own 2027
forecast assumes AI capital spending rises a further 10-20%.

Wars and droughts have rerouted trade more than they have shrunk it. Middle East crude
exports fell about 24% in the first half of 2026, while world container traffic rose 3.9% to
July. Shipping-press summaries put the Panama Canal back at 33 transits a day and Red Sea
traffic still 47% below its level before the Houthi attacks.

Tariffs are the downside case. The US effective tariff rate was 11.0% in April 2026, the
highest since 1943 apart from 2025
([Yale Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/state-us-tariffs-april-2-2026), read
from a summary). Over the tariff years 2018-2024, trade grew only 0.71 times as fast as
output on the panel's arithmetic. The panel gives about one chance in five that the full
twenty years end below 0.75.

## Short-term money stays cheap; long-term money does not

The neutral real interest rate averages about 0.9% in 2036-2045. This is the short-term rate,
after inflation, that neither speeds the economy up nor slows it down. The New York Fed's
estimates put it at 0.73% in mid-2026 across the US, euro area and Canada, and at 0.93% on
average over 2016-2025
([New York Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/policy/rstar)).

The estimate has not followed the bond market. The US 10-year Treasury yield peaked at 5.31%
on 5 October 2026. The inflation-protected 10-year yield stood at 2.60% in early November, up
from 1.93% at the end of 2025 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/DFII10)).

That rise sits in the extra return investors demand for holding long bonds, which the
neutral-rate estimate does not use. The panel expects the gap to last: governments and firms
pay much more to borrow for ten years than the neutral rate alone would suggest.

A return to 2% or more gets 9%. No ten-year stretch starting after 2001 has averaged that
much.

The range runs from just below zero to 2.0%, and both ends are live. Ageing and slower growth
pull the rate down; heavy public borrowing and AI investment push it up.

The estimate itself is fragile. Its stated standard error is 1.5 points for the US and 4.8
for the euro area. One team gave 30% odds that the series is replaced by 2046.

## Debt climbs, and the rules hold

Advanced-economy public debt reaches about 136% of GDP in 2045, up from 108% in 2025. The IMF
already projects 115% by 2031
([IMF](https://www.imf.org/external/datamapper/GGXWDG_NGDP@WEO)).

The panel extends that slope and adds a margin. The IMF finds that debt five years ahead has
run about 10 points of GDP above its projections on average
([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2024/10/15/global-public-debt-is-probably-worse-than-it-looks)),
and twenty years is long enough for at least one crisis-sized jump.

The United States drives the climb. The panel's teams carry US debt to roughly 175% of GDP by
2045, with Japan near 185-190% and Italy near 140%.

| Gross public debt, % of GDP (IMF, October 2026) | 2025 | 2031 projection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Japan | 204.8 | 188.9 |
| United States | 124.4 | 145.0 |
| Italy | 137.1 | 135.6 |
| France | 115.5 | 123.6 |
| Canada | 113.6 | 107.0 |
| United Kingdom | 102.1 | 100.2 |
| Germany | 62.7 | 75.6 |

Policy stays conventional. Three G7 countries adopting a formal yield cap or a large
automation tax gets 3%. Japan and the US are each plausible, at 9-30% and 9-25% across teams.

A third country is the hurdle. Italy sits just under the 140% debt test, and the European
Central Bank would have to name a yield number.

Today's direction is the opposite. The Federal Reserve raised rates in September 2026, and
the Bank of Japan is raising rates and cutting bond purchases. No G7 country has an
automation tax; a US bill,
[H.R. 10044](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr10044ih), has no official revenue
estimate.

The crisis tail is real but small. A G7 default, restructuring or IMF programme in 2036-2045
gets 8-12% across teams. Italy and France are the likeliest, then a missed US payment. A
decade of 4% inflation with growth under 1% adds about 2%.

## Climate costs build slowly in public budgets

Climate change reaches G7 budgets as a slow rise, not a shock. Spending on adaptation,
disaster relief, insurance backstops and household support climbs to perhaps 0.6-0.9% of GDP
a year by 2040-2045, from roughly 0.3% today. Both figures are the panel's own estimates; no
official series exists.

The evidence points one way. Catastrophe losses, public and private together, run at 0.2-0.3%
of world GDP. Swiss Re puts first-half 2026 economic losses near $100 billion, of which $42
billion was insured
([Swiss Re](https://www.swissre.com/institute/research/topics-and-risk-dialogues/climate-and-natural-catastrophe-risk/first-half-2026-insured-catastrophe-losses.html),
read from a summary).

The IMF's figure for extra adaptation investment in advanced economies is about 0.15-0.2% of
GDP a year over 2025-2045
([IMF presentation](https://www.suerf.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Davide-Furceri-Fiscal-Monitor.pdf),
read off a chart). Single events can still be large: Spain's flood reconstruction is reported
at 0.7 points of output over three years.

A burden of 1.5% of GDP a year, five times today's, gets 6%.

The larger effect is on output. This project's economic model puts global climate damage near
2.1% of GDP in 2040-2045 at its default settings, with 1.9°C of warming by 2045. Under its
harsher climate settings damage reaches 4.6-5.5%, and growth in the rich world and China runs
0.12-0.16 points a year slower.

One limit matters here. The panel forecast a world with climate change in it. It did not
measure how different the numbers would be without it.

## The world's economic weights barely move

The US and China together hold about two-fifths of world output in 2045, as they do now.
Their combined share of world GDP in dollars is 42.3% in 2026, and the IMF projects 42.4% for
2031 ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/external/datamapper/NGDPD@WEO)).

Neither is on course to pull away. In volume terms one team has the pair slipping to about
40% by 2045, with the US growing near 1.9% a year and China near 3.2% against a world at
2.7%. Prices and exchange rates would have to supply ten points for the pair to reach half.

Europe drifts down. The EU's share is 17.8% in 2026 and 16.7% in the IMF's 2031 projection.
One team's path puts it near 14% by 2045, still above the 11.5% that would mark an eclipse.

The gains go to the rest of the world. A steady pair and a shrinking Europe leave four or so
points for India and other emerging economies.

## Three ways this could be wrong

The central story is continuity, so the errors that matter are breaks. The panel priced two
of them and could not price the third.

1. **The boom arrives (about 20%).** AI lifts OECD productivity by far more than the 0.3
   points a year the panel's arithmetic allowed, and China holds growth near its official
   targets. Growth per person then passes 2.5% a year in the late 2030s.
2. **The crisis arrives (about 12%).** Bond markets are already strained: press reports have
   the UK 30-year yield above 6% and Japan's 10-year above 3%. A G7 government defaults,
   restructures or turns to the IMF between 2036 and 2045.
3. **The yardsticks move.** Several of these quantities rest on series that may not last
   twenty years: the New York Fed's neutral-rate estimate, the WTO's AI-hardware category, the
   World Bank's price benchmarks for China. The climate-cost question has no official measure
   at all.

One risk sits under all three. The panel's seats are AI models that share training and
sources, so their errors are likely to point the same way.

## What to watch

Seven observable developments would move this forecast, most of them within the next five
years.

| When | What to watch | What it would change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2027-2028 | Whether AI-hardware trade keeps growing once capital spending levels off | Whether trade outruns output or trails it |
| 2027 | Elections in France and Italy, and Italy's debt against 140% of GDP | The crisis tail and the odds of a European yield cap |
| By mid-2028 | Who maintains the New York Fed's neutral-rate estimate when its president's term ends (a second-hand report) | Whether the rate forecast keeps its yardstick |
| From 2031 | China's reported growth: near 3.6% a year or near 2.2% | About 0.5 points a year on growth, and the boom case |
| From 2031 | The slope of US debt: about 2 or about 4 points of GDP a year | A 15-point swing in 2045 advanced-economy debt |
| Any year | A central bank naming a yield number, or an automation tax with an official revenue estimate | The policy long shot stops being one |
| Any year | A first official cross-country estimate of climate costs in budgets | Whether the 0.6-0.9% estimate holds |

## Basis, limits and sources

This forecast was made on 9 November 2026 by a panel of 24 AI forecasting seats. Four teams
(trade, interest rates, public debt, growth) each ran six roles: a base-rate historian, an
arithmetic modeller, a current-evidence scout, a Bayesian updater, a red team and a final
calibrator. The numbers here are simple averages across all 24.

- Each number answers a precisely worded question, and the wording is the panel's own. The
  neutral rate is the New York Fed's estimate, not a market yield. Debt is the IMF's
  advanced-economy group, which runs about 16 points of GDP below the G7 alone.
- The regime odds depend on where the lines are drawn. Crisis means a G7 default,
  restructuring or IMF programme, or a decade of 4% inflation with growth under 1%. Boom means
  growth per person of 2.5% or more a year in 2036-2045.
- AI seats share training and sources, so agreement among them is weak evidence.
- Some inputs came from press reports or search summaries, not original tables. They are
  marked where they appear.
- The questions resolve in 2046-2048. Nothing here has been scored yet.
